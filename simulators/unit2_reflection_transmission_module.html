Layer 1 — Conceptual Understanding

Reflection & Transmission Coefficients at a Dielectric Interface

When a plane EM wave strikes the boundary between two linear dielectric media, part of its energy reflects back and part transmits across — in general at a different angle. How much of each, and how the transmitted ray bends, follows directly from the same boundary conditions on E and H used throughout this course, evaluated at normal incidence and then at an arbitrary angle for the two independent polarizations.

When Do You Get Exactly 100% Transmission or 100% Reflection?

100% Transmission — Γ = 0

Two distinct ways to reach it, and they are not the same thing:

  • Impedance matching, any angle: if — same medium on both sides, or a coating chosen so its impedance equals the surrounding medium's — both TE and TM transmit fully, at every incidence angle. This is the index-matching principle behind AR coatings.
  • Brewster's angle, TM only: at , Γ_TM = 0 exactly — but Γ_TE is generally still non-zero at that same angle. Brewster's angle gives zero reflection only for the TM-polarized component, not for unpolarized light as a whole. Try it in the Simulator: set both media as lossless dielectrics, dial the angle to θ_B from the readout table, and watch only the magenta curve touch zero.

100% Reflection — |Γ| = 1

Two distinct routes here too:

  • Total internal reflection: both media lossless dielectrics, , angle beyond the critical angle — exact, for both polarizations, no energy lost.
  • Reflection off a conductor: switch medium 2 to Conductor — |Γ| approaches 1 at any angle and any polarization, exactly reaching 1 only in the idealized perfect-conductor limit (σ→∞); a real conductor like copper gets to ≈99.99%, with the tiny remainder absorbed in the skin depth, not transmitted onward.

Which One Do Real-Time Systems Actually Need?

Both — deliberately, depending on the job. Systems that must pass a signal through a boundary are engineered toward Γ→0: this module's radar-radome and solar-AR-coating Applications are exactly that case — reflection is a loss to be minimized. Systems that must keep a signal from crossing a boundary are engineered toward |Γ|→1 instead: optical fibres rely on total internal reflection to guide light with almost no loss over kilometres, radar corner reflectors and mirrors depend on it for a strong return signal, and shielding enclosures/waveguide walls use conductor boundaries specifically because they reflect almost everything. There is rarely a reason to design for anything in between — partial reflection is usually just wasted signal, which is why real engineering designs are pushed deliberately toward one extreme or the other.

NUMERICAL DATA

Physical Principle: Bare silicon (n≈3.67) is a huge index mismatch from air (n=1), so a direct air–silicon interface reflects a large fraction of incident sunlight. Inserting a coating whose index sits between air and silicon (e.g. silicon nitride, n≈2.0) reduces that mismatch at the outer surface.

Mathematical Connection: Both surfaces are scored with the same single-interface reflection coefficient — comparing air→silicon directly against air→coating shows quantitatively how much index-matching alone reduces reflectance, even before accounting for the additional thin-film interference effect a real quarter-wave coating adds on top.

Engineering Relevance: This is the starting design principle behind every anti-reflective coating on solar cells and camera lenses: pick a coating index close to the geometric mean of the two media it sits between, , to minimize the reflection coefficient at that interface.

Key Takeaway: Even without invoking interference (which needs a coating-thickness parameter beyond this model's scope), index-matching alone cuts front-surface reflection loss substantially — real AR coatings then add a quarter-wave thickness to cancel the remaining reflection by interference.
